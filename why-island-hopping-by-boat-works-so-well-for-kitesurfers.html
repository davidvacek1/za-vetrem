Why Island-Hopping by Boat Works So Well for Kitesurfers

A kite holiday based on one beach depends on that beach for the whole week. When the wind angle turns awkward or the water gets crowded, the choice is often to wait or drive to another spot. A yacht removes that limit. In the sea off Hurghada, several islands with sandy lagoons lie within reach of each other, and a boat that carries the group, the gear and the kitchen can move to whichever one fits the day's forecast.

Shallow lagoons and what lies around them For a rider, the useful part of each island is the water around it, and that water is where the islands off Hurghada differ most. Some lagoons are shallow over sand. Others sit next to deeper water and reefs. Which lagoon works on a given day depends on the riders on board as much as on the wind.

Before the first launch at a new lagoon, it is worth asking where the depth changes and what the bottom is made of. A short read on how lagoon depth shapes kite safari safety covers those checks.

Picking an island by the water you want The islands are easier to compare by the riding they offer than by name. For calm, shallow water over sand there is Abu Minqar, close to Hurghada and handy for practising tricks, or the big turquoise lagoon at Tawila, where the organiser gives 15 to 25 knots (8 to 13 m/s); a visit there is paid and has to be agreed ahead. Riders after waves have Geisum, with its long beach and a mix of shallow and deeper water, and remote Ashrafi, which suits riders who already feel confident. Gubal has stronger wind, deeper water and coral reefs, and the boat heads there only for guests who want to dive.

With a steady north-westerly for most of the year, each island's shape decides which side is good for launching and what lies downwind. A guide to island conditions on a Red Sea kite safari shows where the islands sit and how far apart they are.

A day built around the forecast On a boat, the question of where to ride that day is not the rider's to solve. The crew reads the forecast in the morning and takes the yacht to the island that fits it. Nobody loads a car or drives to a second spot when the first one turns out flat. Riders hear the plan at the morning briefing.

How the rest of the week fits around the sessions, from arrival to the last day, is described in how a liveaboard kite safari week runs.

Downwinders between islands Island-hopping also opens up point-to-point riding. Several of the northern islands lie close together, so a run that starts in one lagoon can end at the next island instead of in open water. From Geisum, riders can go downwind towards the next islands, and dolphins are sometimes seen around Geisum. The wind direction on the day decides which neighbour is within reach.

Downwinders are part of the programme for riders who are past the course stage. Before joining one, it helps to understand pairs, spacing, route choice and what an offshore stretch means near an island. The basics are covered in planning downwinders on a kite safari.
