How should a kiter read wind data before booking a trip?

Planning a kite trip means looking at wind on three clocks. Months before departure you choose a season from climate records. A few days out you start trusting forecasts. On the morning itself you pick a kite. Each step uses different numbers, and a common packing mistake is carrying a figure from one step into another where it no longer fits.

Months ahead: picking the season No forecast reaches far enough to book a flight, so the first decision rests on long-term records. For a point off Hurghada, NASA POWER data for 2001 to 2025 show the northern Red Sea blowing from the north-west in every month, while the strength shifts: June to September average about 13 to 14 knots (6.8 to 7.4 m/s), November drops to about 10 knots (5.3 m/s). Single years wander too. In the same series, October has ranged from under 9 knots (4.6 m/s) to over 14 knots (7.3 m/s).

So the season tells you what is likely, and the actual week can still surprise you. Hotter months can be windier on paper and still less comfortable on deck, and boat dates also depend on when the yacht is chartered. The seasonal wind pattern behind kite safari dates is worth reading month by month before you settle on a week.

What the monthly number hides A long-term October mean of 12 knots (6.2 m/s) points to a large light-wind kite. It is a 24-hour figure over a model grid cell tens of kilometres wide, so it counts night hours and calm spells, and averages open sea with desert. A lagoon tucked behind an island can behave quite differently in the riding hours. One Red Sea liveaboard organiser puts Tawila lagoon at a steady 15 to 25 knots (8 to 13 m/s), well above the grid-cell mean.

Read a monthly figure as the middle of a wide spread. How grid averages and afternoon breezes relate is laid out in a piece on kite safari wind averages versus afternoon thermals.

Days ahead: one unit for every number Once forecasts appear, the risk changes. Climate tables, apps and weather stations often use different units, and most kite size charts expect knots. One knot is 0.514 m/s, so 1 m/s is close to 2 knots.

Take a 68 kg rider whose app shows "8". If that is 8 m/s, the wind is about 15.6 knots, and the common rule of 2.2 × weight ÷ knots gives roughly 9.6 m². Read the same 8 as knots (4.1 m/s) and the answer jumps to almost 19 m², a kite twice too large. A short guide to wind units for kite safari forecasts covers knots, m/s, km/h and Beaufort forces side by side.

From numbers to a quiver The weight rule turns a wind band into a packing list. For the same 68 kg rider on a twintip:

  • 15 knots (7.7 m/s): about 10 m²
  • 20 knots (10.3 m/s): about 7.5 m²
  • 25 knots (12.9 m/s): about 6 m²

Two kites near 10 and 7 m² cover most of that band. Sizing from the monthly mean instead would suggest a 12 or 13 m² kite, useful only on the lightest afternoons. Gusts, board type, rider level and kite model all shift the answer, which is why the formula is a starting point. The reasoning behind the kite size rule for a kite safari explains where it holds and where to adjust.

On the day, size for the lighter start of the session: with 15 to 20 knots (7.7 to 10.3 m/s) forecast, that rider rigs the 10 m² kite and changes down as the wind builds.
